## Tuesday 2026-08-25 — Article: "File vs. Graph: What a LinkedIn Export Actually Becomes"

People sometimes ask why not just paste a LinkedIn export into an AI chat and start asking it
questions, instead of building anything. It's a fair question, and the honest answer isn't
"because I sell the alternative." Both approaches start from the exact same export. What
happens next is the whole difference.

**Privacy tiering**

A raw zip is one unsorted pile. Messages, phone numbers, ad-targeting data, job applications —
all in or all out. Nothing you'd actually want to hand a recruiter, or anyone else, in one shot.

Run through a real pipeline, that same export splits into tiers: a public-safe subset, and a
private tier gated behind a token that adds career signals on top. You choose what's shared
before anyone asks.

**Evidence grounding**

Uploaded raw, an AI re-parses LinkedIn's CSV format cold, every single conversation, with no
signal for what's actually proven versus what's just saved. A bookmarked job posting and an
earned certification sit in the same undifferentiated pile.

A real pipeline tags every entry demonstrated or signal_only. A saved job alert can never
masquerade as a skill.

**Search quality**

A generic document upload gets generic chunking — not tuned for this shape of data. A
purpose-built pipeline uses embedding search with a similarity floor set empirically against the
model's real score distribution, plus an exhaustive lookup for straightforward "how many"
questions a similarity search handles badly.

**Portability**

A zip in a Project lives in one Project, one account. Want it somewhere else, you re-upload it.
A real server is one URL, reachable from any MCP-aware client, no re-upload, ever.

**Size and noise**

A raw export includes everything, including what nobody would ever ask about. A real pipeline
pre-filters and deduplicates down to what's actually queryable knowledge.

**Relationship traversal**

A raw export is just rows of text. Ask what's related to something and the AI re-reads
everything from scratch, guessing at connections nothing ever recorded.

A real pipeline walks actual graph edges — shared tag or provider — so a course surfaces the
endorsements it actually backs, not just ones that happen to share wording.

**Depth signal**

A raw export has no sense of scale. A one-off bookmark and years of provable work read the same
length in a chat — there's no count behind either.

The same graph-traversal call reports the real, uncapped count behind a tag or provider: 3
entries versus 120 is a visibly different depth, which is exactly the signal for whether new
content on that topic is still worth your time.

**In real numbers**

A genuine 54-file LinkedIn export, run through the real pipeline: 2,525 raw connections in the
zip, a 4.7 MB messages.csv alone, versus 109 public-tier entries and 160 private-tier entries
kept after filtering and dedup, matched at a 0.65 similarity floor.

**Bottom line**

Uploading the zip to a Project is faster to set up, and that's genuinely fine for a private,
one-off check on your own account. It gives up three things the moment you'd want to share it,
reuse it elsewhere, or trust it not to cite a saved search as a skill: privacy tiering, evidence
discipline, and portability.

"How do I tailor my CV for this job without burning half a day on it?"

That's the actual question — not "which AI should write my resume for me." I asked myself this
too many times, rereading a posting, guessing which of ten years of bullet points to lead with,
then doing it all again for the next one.

So I built a matcher instead of doing it by hand. Feed it a job posting: it checks which
requirements my real CV bullets already back — exact term matches first, then a semantic pass
for bullets that clearly support a requirement in different words (a posting wants "data
governance," a bullet says "standardised dataset, single source of truth"). Every semantic match
ships with its real bullet text and a similarity score, labeled "verify before using" — never
silently promoted to a fact.

The part that actually matters: it only ever selects and reorders bullets that are already true.
It never invents a sentence to close a gap. If a requirement has nothing behind it, it says so —
an honest gap, not a smoothed-over one.

Two real applications have gone out through it so far. Same rule as everywhere else in this
project: never claim something as proven that isn't.

## Thursday/Friday 2026-08-27/28 — standalone post: "The schema decision"

Every learning-history source I ingest — LinkedIn, GitHub, course platforms, Gmail completion
emails — writes to the exact same six fields: title, type, provider, date, description,
domain_tags.

That's the whole schema. No LinkedIn-specific table, no GitHub-specific one.

Seven ingest scripts exist today, one per source, and each is the only thing that has to know
its source's raw format. Everything downstream — dedup, graph-building — reads the common shape
and doesn't care where an entry came from.

The payoff shows up when a new source gets added: no changes to the merge or graph-building
logic, just one more script writing the same six fields. Source #8 costs the same to add as
source #2 did.
